Universal Chemistry Calculator

Every calculation in DTU 26022 Kemi, in one file that works with the network switched off. Start at the index and search for whatever the question is about.

Tutorial six things worth knowing before the exam
  1. One box at the top drives most of the app. Type a formula there and the Composition, Name and Solutions tabs all describe it. Type an equation instead and Balance, Stoichiometry, Equilibrium, Kinetics and the enthalpy tools all pick it up. Press / anywhere to jump back to it.
  2. Leave the unknown empty. Nearly every calculator works the same way: fill in what the question gives you, leave the one you want blank, and it is worked out. Change your mind and clear a different box instead; the one you left empty stays the answer.
  3. Every field carries its own unit. Grams, moles, atm, kPa, mL, kJ/mol, mM, °C, whatever the question uses. You never have to convert by hand, and a stoichiometry row can be in moles while the row below it is in grams.
  4. The working is shown, not just the answer. Each tool prints the substitution it made, so you can copy the reasoning onto your paper and check it against your own. If an answer disagrees with a key, the working usually shows which step differs.
  5. It tells you when it is approximating. The small-x shortcut, average bond energies, the least-electronegative-atom rule and predicted states are all flagged. Where a standard assumption would not have been valid, it says so rather than quietly using it.
  6. Nothing is loaded from the network. Every table is inside this one file: atomic weights, Appendix G, bond energies, Ksp, Ka and Kb, standard potentials, specific heats. Open it with the wifi off and it behaves identically.
A whole question, start to finish

Say the paper asks: what is the maximum theoretical yield of SnI4 from 5.0 g of tin and 9.0 g of iodine?

  1. Search the index for a phrase from the question, such as theoretical yield. It lands on Stoichiometry and limiting reactant.
  2. Put Sn + I2 -> SnI4 in the box at the top. It balances itself and the rows appear.
  3. Set both reactant rows to g, type 5.0 and 9.0, and read off the limiting reactant, the leftover and the theoretical yield.
  4. If the question then asks for a percent yield, type the actual mass into the product row.

The pattern is the same everywhere: search a phrase, load the reaction, fill in what you were given, leave the unknown blank.

If something looks wrong

Check the working line first. The commonest causes are a stale equation still sitting in the top box, a unit dropdown left on the wrong setting, or a Lewis structure where the question intended a different central atom, which you can override. Where the app and a printed answer key disagree, the key has more often been the one rounding early.

Ideal gas law

Leave the box you want calculated empty. PV = nRT. Amount can be given as moles or as a mass, in which case the molar mass comes from the formula at the top of the page.

Real gases: van der Waals

(P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT. The a term corrects for attraction between molecules, which lowers the pressure; the b term corrects for the volume the molecules themselves occupy, which raises it.

The individual gas laws

Boyle, Charles, Amontons and Avogadro are all the combined gas law with something held constant: P₁V₁/n₁T₁ = P₂V₂/n₂T₂. Fill in what changes and leave the unknown blank; anything you leave out entirely is treated as constant and the law being used is named for you.

One unit per quantity, used on both sides. For pressure, volume and amount only the ratio matters, so the unit cancels; temperature is converted to kelvin first, which is why a Celsius scale would otherwise give the wrong answer.

Gas density and molar mass

Ionic crystals and X-ray diffraction

In a rock-salt structure the ions alternate along the cell edge, so a = 2(r+ + r−). If the cation is small enough that the anions touch each other instead, they meet along the face diagonal, which gives r− = a√2/4.

Bragg's law

nλ = 2d sinθ. X-rays reflected from successive planes of atoms reinforce each other only when the extra path is a whole number of wavelengths. Leave one box empty.

Following a reaction by pressure

At fixed volume and temperature, pressure counts molecules. If a known amount of A reacts completely with an excess of B, the pressures before and after give the mole ratio, and with it the formula.

Coupled equilibria: K for a combined reaction

Buffer: Henderson and Hasselbalch

pH = pKa + log([base]/[acid]). Amounts may be moles or concentrations, since only their ratio matters.

Adding strong acid or base, and what it would have done to plain water

Strong base converts acid into its conjugate base, one for one; strong acid does the reverse. Neither changes the total, so only the ratio moves, and the ratio sits inside a logarithm. That is the whole mechanism. The same addition to unbuffered water at the same starting pH is worked out alongside, because the comparison is the point of the question.

The acid and base amounts above are read as moles here, since adding something needs real quantities rather than a ratio. If you entered them as concentrations, multiply by the buffer volume first.

Titration of a weak acid with a strong base

Give the acid and the titrant, then a volume of base added. It picks the right regime for that point: pure acid, buffer, equivalence, or excess base.

Solubility product

Type a slightly soluble salt and it writes the dissolution equation and the Ksp expression, then works out the molar solubility. A common ion concentration lowers it.

Solubility and pH

A salt dissolves better in acid whenever its anion is a base. The acid protonates that anion, removing it from solution, and the dissolution equilibrium slides right to replace it. Two things follow, and they are the whole point: a salt of a strong-acid anion such as Cl− or NO3− is untouched by pH, and in a buffer the pH is held fixed, so the anion concentration is pinned by the buffer rather than tracking the solubility.

The Ka boxes are filled in for you when the anion is a familiar one, but they are ordinary inputs: type your own and the tool works for any salt. Leave them empty for a hydroxide, whose OH− is already set by the pH, or for a strong-acid anion, which does not protonate at all.

If the pH comes from a buffer

Will a precipitate form?

Work out Q the same way as Ksp, but with the concentrations you actually have. Q above Ksp means a solid appears and keeps appearing until Q falls back to Ksp; Q below it means everything stays dissolved.

Complex ions and dissolving a precipitate

A metal ion and a ligand join into a complex ion, and the formation constant Kf says how far that goes. Kf is usually enormous, so nearly all the metal ends up bound and the concentration of free metal ion collapses. That is what lets a complexing agent dissolve a precipitate the water alone would never touch.

Leave the ligand added box empty if what you made up was the complex ion itself, dissolved in pure water: then the only ligand present is what the complex releases as it falls apart, and you are looking at its dissociation. A polyatomic ligand needs a space before its charge, as in S2O3 2-, because S2O32- could be read either way.

Dissolving a precipitate with a complexing agent

The two equilibria add: the solid dissolves, and the metal it releases is captured by the ligand. Adding equations multiplies their constants, so K = Ksp × Kf. A Ksp of 10−13 against a Kf of 1013 gives an overall K near 1, which is why the solid goes into solution. It uses the ligand, n and Kf set above, so pick the complex there first.

Heat capacity and q = mcΔT

Leave the box you want calculated empty. The Substance list is a lookup: choosing one copies its tabulated specific heat into the c box for you. If your substance is not listed, leave that dropdown alone and type the value into c yourself, and the app will say which tabulated substance that value belongs to. The heat capacity of the sample, C = mc, appears below and needs only a mass and a specific heat, with no temperature change at all.

Calorimetry, two bodies reaching one temperature

Leave the box you want calculated empty. No heat escapes, so what one body loses the other gains: m1c1(Tf − T1) + m2c2(Tf − T2) = 0. Specific heats are in J/(g·°C) and temperatures in °C. Leaving a specific heat blank is the standard "identify the unknown metal" question: the value comes back with a suggestion of what the metal is.

Bomb and coffee-cup calorimeter

Leave the box you want calculated empty. Two things absorb the heat: the calorimeter hardware, with heat capacity Ccal in J/°C, and its contents, usually water. So q = (Ccal + m c) ΔT. Leave either one blank if your problem does not have it. For water, millilitres and grams are numerically the same, so 675 mL is 675 g.

ΔG, K and the temperature where it turns

ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, and ΔG° = −RT ln K. Enter ΔH and ΔS from the section above, or type your own, and the crossover temperature is where ΔG changes sign.

Hess's law, combining given reactions

One reaction per line as equation | ΔH, then the reaction you want underneath. It works out what to multiply each step by, and whether to reverse it, then adds them up. Fractional coefficients such as 3/2 or 1.5 are fine, and state symbols count, so H2O(l) and H2O(g) are treated as different substances.

Enthalpy of reaction from formation enthalpies

ΔH°rxn = Σ nΔH°f(products) − Σ mΔH°f(reactants). Put a reaction in the box at the top of the page, then type each ΔH°f in kJ/mol from the appendix. An element in its standard state is zero, so those rows can be left blank. The Appendix G column carries the whole of OpenStax appendix G, 246 substances. Photon: wavelength, frequency, energy

Type into any one of the first five boxes and the rest follow. These are one quantity in different clothes, tied by c = λν and E = hν = hc/λ. Adding a total energy also gives the number of photons needed to deliver it, which pairs with a q from the Thermo tab.

Photoelectric effect

Leave the box you want calculated empty. Wavelength and frequency are the same thing, so filling either one counts as knowing the incident light. KE = hν − Φ. A photon either has enough energy to free an electron or it does not, however intense the light.

Bohr model, hydrogen-like atoms

En = −kZ²/n² with k = 2.179 × 10-18 J. An electron dropping from ni to nf emits the difference as one photon.

de Broglie wavelength

λ = h/(mv). Matter has a wavelength too; it is only measurable when the mass is tiny.

Quantum numbers and subshells

Fill in as much as the question gives you. With n and l you get the subshell; add ml and ms and the whole set is checked against the rules. The table underneath lists everything allowed for that n.

Degenerate means equal in energy. In a multi-electron atom only the orbitals within one subshell are degenerate, so 3s, 3p and 3d all differ. In hydrogen, with a single electron and no electron-electron repulsion, every subshell of the same n has the same energy, so 3s, 3p and 3d are all degenerate together: 9 orbitals in one set.

Combustion analysis

Burning a C, H, O compound in excess oxygen puts every carbon into CO2 and every hydrogen into H2O. Oxygen is then found by difference from the sample mass.

Solubility

Type an ionic formula to see whether it dissolves in water, which is what decides if a double-replacement reaction gives a precipitate. Several salts separated by commas get a verdict each. Cations, then a semicolon, then anions (Na+, Ca2+, Pb2+ ; Cl-, SO4 2-) gives a grid of which anion precipitates which cation.

Standard atomic weights are IUPAC conventional values. Values in [brackets] are the mass number of the most stable known isotope, since those elements have no stable nuclide.

Accepts Al2(SO4)3, K4[Fe(CN)6], CuSO4·5H2O (also written CuSO4*5H2O or CuSO4.5H2O), subscripts like H₂O, and a leading coefficient like 3Ca3(PO4)2. Symbols are case sensitive: Co is cobalt, CO is carbon monoxide.

Mass units use exact definitions: 1 lb = 453.59237 g, 1 oz = 28.349523125 g, 1 gr = 0.06479891 g, 1 ton = 2000 lb, 1 t = 106 g. OpenStax rounds the pound to 454 g, so an answer taken from the book may differ from this one in the third significant figure.

The Name tab covers the five patterns in OpenStax chapter 2: binary ionic, ionic with polyatomic ions, acids, hydrates, and binary molecular compounds, plus double salts. It works out a variable metal's charge from the anion, so Fe2O3 gives iron(III). It does not do organic or coordination-complex naming, and it flags formulas whose charges fail to balance rather than guessing.

The Balance tab solves the atom and charge conservation equations exactly, using fractions rather than floating point, so the coefficients it returns are the true smallest whole numbers. It handles redox by balancing charge as an extra conservation law, which means an ionic equation balances correctly provided you list every species including H^+, OH^-, H2O or e^- as needed. If an equation has more than one independent solution it says so instead of picking one arbitrarily.

The Atoms tab covers chapter 2.3 and 2.5: protons, neutrons and electrons from an isotope symbol, periodic table position, and the abundance-weighted average atomic mass. The Name to formula box under the naming tables runs chapter 2.7 in reverse, cross-multiplying the ion charges rather than looking the answer up.
